## Name

compression_calibration_drift

## Title

Does Compression Break Calibration? A Controlled Study of Quantization and Pruning Effects on Confidence Reliability in Tiny Networks

## Short Hypothesis

Post-training quantization and pruning of tiny neural networks systematically shift confidence calibration (typically toward overconfidence) in a way that is predictable from the compression ratio, and a single cheap post-hoc temperature refit after compression is sufficient to restore near-original calibration even though it cannot restore lost accuracy. If true, this decouples 'calibration cost' from 'accuracy cost' of compression and gives a nearly-free recipe for deploying compressed tiny models with reliable confidences.

## Related Work

Prior work on calibration of small models studies temperature scaling, label smoothing, ensembles, and mixup in isolation from model compression (as in our own related tiny-model calibration study). Separately, the LLM literature has studied how calibration DATA used during post-training quantization affects downstream task accuracy (Williams & Aletras, 2023), but this is about choosing calibration examples for the compression algorithm itself, not about the calibration (confidence reliability) of the resulting model's predictions -- a completely different sense of 'calibration.' We are not aware of controlled small-scale studies that measure expected calibration error (ECE) as a function of quantization bit-width and pruning sparsity in tiny classifiers, nor of work testing whether a single post-compression temperature refit suffices to correct any induced miscalibration. This distinguishes our proposal from both bodies of work: we study the intersection of model compression and confidence calibration (not calibration data selection for compression, and not calibration methods studied without compression).

## Abstract

Efficient on-device inference relies on compressing already-small models via quantization and pruning, but little is known about how these operations affect the reliability of predicted confidences, as distinct from accuracy. We hypothesize that quantization and pruning of tiny neural networks (under one million parameters) induce a predictable calibration drift, generally toward overconfidence as decision boundaries are coarsened by reduced weight precision or sparsified connectivity, and that this drift can be corrected almost for free by refitting a single temperature scalar on a small held-out set after compression, without any retraining. We test this on tiny MLPs and CNNs trained on MNIST, FashionMNIST, and UCI tabular datasets, applying post-training quantization (int8, int4, int2 weights) and unstructured magnitude pruning (30-90% sparsity) across a grid of compression levels. For each configuration we measure expected calibration error (ECE), Brier score, and accuracy before and after a post-compression temperature refit, and we track whether the optimal temperature scales monotonically with compression ratio (allowing prediction of the right temperature from compression level alone, without a validation set). We further test whether calibration drift and its correctability persist under distribution shift (image corruptions, label noise) and whether label smoothing or mixup during original training makes models more robust to compression-induced miscalibration. The result is a controlled empirical map of calibration cost as a function of compression, plus a practical, near-zero-cost recipe (refit-temperature-after-compression) for deploying compressed tiny models with trustworthy confidence estimates, all runnable in minutes on CPU or a single small GPU.

## Experiments

(1) Train baseline tiny models: 2-3 layer MLPs (<100K params) on UCI tabular tasks (e.g. Adult, Covertype subsample) and small CNNs (<1M params) on MNIST/FashionMNIST, subsampled to <=10K training examples, with standard cross-entropy loss. (2) Apply post-training quantization to weights (and optionally activations) at int8, int4, int2 precision using simple uniform quantization (no need for advanced PTQ libraries -- implement min-max quantization manually). (3) Apply unstructured magnitude pruning at sparsity levels {30%,50%,70%,90%} with one-shot fine-tuning-free pruning. (4) For each of the resulting ~20-30 (quantization x pruning) configurations, measure test accuracy, ECE (15-bin), Brier score, and reliability diagrams both before and after refitting a single temperature scalar (fit by minimizing NLL on a small held-out calibration split of 500-1000 examples). (5) Test the 'monotonic temperature' hypothesis: regress optimal post-compression temperature against a simple scalar compression metric (e.g., bits-per-weight x (1-sparsity)) and report R^2; if strong, this supports predicting temperature without a calibration set. (6) Repeat under distribution shift: corrupt test images (Gaussian noise, blur, brightness at 3 severities) and inject symmetric label noise (5%, 10%) into a fraction of training data, re-measuring whether the pre-compression temperature-refit recipe still restores calibration post-shift, or whether shift and compression interact non-additively. (7) Ablation: repeat the full pipeline for models originally trained with label smoothing (alpha=0.1) or mixup, testing whether these training-time interventions make models more robust to compression-induced miscalibration (smaller drift, smaller temperature correction needed). (8) Report all results as heatmaps of ECE over the (bit-width, sparsity) grid, before/after refit, plus the temperature-vs-compression regression fit.

## Risk Factors And Limitations

Unstructured pruning at high sparsity without fine-tuning may collapse accuracy so much that calibration metrics become uninformative (need to report accuracy alongside ECE and possibly exclude degenerate configurations). Simple manual quantization may not reflect real deployed quantization kernels (e.g., per-channel quantization, activation quantization nuances); results may not transfer directly to production quantization toolchains. The 'monotonic temperature' relationship may not hold cleanly across model architectures or datasets, weakening the practical recipe claim -- this would still be a valid and useful negative result but changes the paper's contribution from a prescriptive recipe to a diagnostic map. Single temperature scaling only corrects global miscalibration and may fail to capture class-conditional or subgroup miscalibration introduced by compression, which the study does not directly address unless extended with class-wise ECE. Findings from MNIST/FashionMNIST/UCI-scale tasks may not generalize to more complex data distributions.
